# WP-04 — Transport spike: Route A vs Route B

**Stream:** hardware · **Owner:** Hardware Lead designs, Michael prints and ranks
**Status:** packet WP04-01 built, waiting on Q-006 · **Depends on:** Q-006
**Blocks:** WP-20, WP-22 — and WP-22 is the six-to-ten-week long pole

## Interface

A latching five-button transport with a Walkman interlock: stop pops play, record will
not stay down without play held, end-of-tape pops play under firmware control.

Off-the-shelf latching switches cannot be released externally — they latch on an internal
heart-cam and only a second press frees them. So:

- **Route A (preferred)** — momentary switches under printed carriers latching on a shared
  hook bar. One solenoid pulls the bar, everything pops.
- **Route B (fallback)** — push-push switches with one small solenoid per latching button.

Packet WP04-01 is Route A. Route B needs no printed mechanism to speak of, so it is
evaluated on the bench from order 1b rather than on a plate.

### One argument against Route B is already retired

The charter counts "four solenoids' worth of peak current" against Route B.
`spec/hw/thermal-budget.md` §6 shows that objection costs two logic packages to remove:
**staggering the four firings by 10 ms holds peak current at one solenoid's worth** over a
120 ms sequence nobody can perceive, and the one-shots make the stagger a hardware property
rather than a firmware promise.

So Route A should win on mechanism and on feel, or it should not win. **It should not be
chosen because of a current argument that is not true.** Recorded here so the spike is judged
on the right axis.

### Why merged, and why one orientation

A single merged STL is what forces staff's orientation choice to apply **uniformly**. A blind comparative test survives every variant being rotated *together* — the comparison still means something. It does not survive parts being rotated *differently from each other*, and the on-its-side variant was the only thing making the batch non-uniform.

The card asks the staff for exactly one thing: *"any orientation is acceptable, provided all parts are printed in the same one."* That costs them nothing and it is the only setting protecting the experiment.

### The on-its-side variant is replaced, not just deleted

`X` tested print anisotropy. `Z` now tests **beam compliance** — the barb sits on a cantilever tongue, and `Z` has a longer, thinner one at the same hook depth. Same count, same bracketing, same "is my swept parameter the right one?" role.

This is better than a workaround. **Print anisotropy is a manufacturing choice we control in production, not a design variable Michael should be feeling with his thumb** — asking him to compare a part that differs by print direction confounds the question we actually want answered, which is what beam geometry feels right. And it removes a dependency on a setting nobody in this project controls.

The card asks it blind: *"which letter, if any, feels springier?"* If he picks `Z` out of nine, the tongue is the dominant lever and packet 02 sweeps beam geometry instead of hook depth. If he cannot, hook depth is the right variable.

**Consequence for the CAD:** the carrier now has a real cantilever — `beam_length`, `beam_thickness`, `root_fillet` — where before the barb sat on a rigid stem and nothing flexed. The return spring moved from a bore up the middle of the stem into a pocket in the frame, because the bore and the relief slot wanted the same volume and the spring does not care which part holds it.

### Three controls hidden in the ranking

`M`, `D` and `H` are the **same geometry** (1.2 mm), placed at spread bed positions and
lettered as though they were variants. Michael ranks them blind alongside the rest. This
tests the print and the ranker at once:

- **The three cluster** → bed position is not confounding the sweep, and the ranking is
  trustworthy.
- **They scatter** → print noise is larger than the swept parameter. The next sweep needs
  *coarser* steps, not finer ones — which is the opposite of the instinct after an
  inconclusive result, and the reason the controls are there.

`X` is a fourth copy printed on its side. It is not ranked. Upright printing puts the barb
root on a layer interface (the weak direction) but puts hook depth in the XY plane where FDM
accuracy is best — and hook depth is the thing being measured, so upright is right for the
sweep. `X` is how we find out what that cost.

## The next packet, pre-planned

The charter's fourth packet element, and the one that turns one trip a week into progress
rather than a week of latency per decision. **Every branch below is already decided**, so
the moment `RESULTS.md` comes back, plate two goes out without another round of thinking.

| If WP04-01 says… | Then WP04-02 is… | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **`Z` is picked out as springier** | **Sweep the beam, not the hook.** Five tongues from 6–16 mm at fixed hook depth | Compliance is the dominant lever and depth was the wrong variable |
| A clear winner, controls cluster | **Refine.** Five variants at ±0.15 mm around the winner, plus lead-angle at two values. Same blind protocol. | The bracket worked; tighten it |
| A clear winner, controls **scatter** | **Re-bracket coarser.** Four variants at 0.6 / 1.2 / 1.8 / 2.4 mm, two copies of each at opposite corners. | The parameter is inside the noise; widen until it is not |
| "They all felt the same" | **Hook depth is not the variable.** Sweep the *lead angle* and the *undercut* instead, at fixed 1.2 mm depth. | Depth is not what the thumb feels — the cam ramp is |
| Nothing latches | **Fit, not feel.** Sweep `carrier_clearance` 0.10–0.40 mm at fixed geometry, and thicken the bar. | A mechanism that does not work has no feel to rank |
| Everything latches, nothing pops | **Undercut is too aggressive.** Sweep the shelf angle from undercut through neutral to positive. | The shelf is gripping rather than releasing |
| Hooks snapping off | **Route B moves to preferred**, and WP04-02 becomes a Route B carrier + solenoid bench test. | If the barb cannot survive PLA it will struggle in service, and the fallback is cheap |

Each of these is one changed number in `hardware/cad/transport/latch.py` and a rebuild.
That is the whole return on code-defined CAD (ADR-004).

## Acceptance criteria

| # | Criterion | Belongs to |
|---|---|---|
| A-1 | One printed carrier latches and pops on command **200 times without a failure** | WP-04, this packet, in PLA |
| A-2 | The winning geometry is chosen from a blind ranking, with the controls clustering | WP-04 |
| A-3 | Route A vs Route B logged to `DECISIONS.md` with the cost of reversing it | WP-04 |
| A-4 | **1000 cycles in the final material** (MJF nylon or PETG, service bureau) | **WP-22, not here** |

**A-1 and A-4 are different experiments and must not be conflated** (PM Decisions 001 §7).
200 cycles in PLA is a "does it latch reliably" check and it is roughly fifteen minutes of
thumb. It bounds nothing about life. PLA creeps under sustained spring preload and will
relax the very geometry being tuned, so a PLA durability number would be actively
misleading. A-4 runs on the winning shape only, in the material that ships, and needs the
cycling rig below.

### The cycling rig

1000 cycles by hand is not a thing to ask of anyone. A solenoid, a MOSFET and a counter —
all in order 1b — driven by the Teensy makes it an overnight job on Michael's desk. The rig
prints in the same packet as the mechanism it tests. Deferred to WP-22 because there is no
point building it before there is a winning geometry to feed it.
